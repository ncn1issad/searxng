Self-recognition in an Asian elephant

Joshua M. Plotnik, Frans B. M. de Waal, Diana Reiss (2006) Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 103 (45) 17053-17057 DOI: 10.1073/pnas.0608062103

Mirror self-recognition is regarded as a hallmark of self-awareness and higher-order cognition, previously demonstrated systematically only in humans and great apes. Here we report an Asian elephant passing the mirror mark test.

PDF HTML
Mirror self-recognition is regarded as a hallmark of self-awareness and higher-order cognition, previously demonstrated systematically only in humans and great apes. Here we report an Asian elephant passing the mirror mark test. In front of an 8x8 foot mirror, an elephant named Happy repeatedly applied self-directed trunk touches to an invisible sham mark and a visible painted mark above her right eye, without tactile cues, demonstrating convergent cognitive evolution between primates and proboscideans.

Infrasonic calls in African elephants: frequency characteristics and ground propagation

P. O. Poole, K. Payne, W. R. Langbauer, C. J. Moss (1988) J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 83 (S1) S80 DOI: 10.1121/1.2025732

Field recordings in Amboseli National Park confirmed low-frequency acoustic emissions between 14 Hz and 24 Hz, below human hearing thresholds, enabling long-range coordination among scattered herds.

HTML
Field recordings in Amboseli National Park confirmed low-frequency acoustic emissions between 14 Hz and 24 Hz, below human hearing thresholds. Seismic ground attenuation measurements showed that ground roll Rayleigh waves generated by these rumbles propagate up to 16 km, providing a secondary acoustic channel through elephant footpad mechanoreceptors.

Can elephants crack the mirror test? Critical re-examination and replication attempts

M. R. Gallagher, E. H. Thorne (2023) arXiv:2308.11429 [q-bio.NC] DOI: 10.48550/arXiv.2308.11429

We review three replication attempts of the mirror self-recognition protocol across Asian and African elephant facilities, examining whether methodological differences in mirror size and tactile distraction account for varying success rates.

PDF HTML
We review three replication attempts of the mirror self-recognition protocol across Asian and African elephant facilities. While earlier experiments highlighted individual success, variable outcomes suggest that tactile, olfactory, and motor modalities dominate elephant self-awareness far more than purely visual feedback, requiring cross-modal testing frameworks.

Elephants know when they need a helping hand: cooperation and partner awareness in Asian elephants during loose-string pulling tasks

Joshua M. Plotnik, Richard Lair, Wirot Suphachoksahakun, Frans B. M. de Waal (2011) Proc. Natl. Acad. Sci. USA 108 (12) 5116-5121 DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1101765108

Using a modified cooperative apparatus, pairs of Asian elephants were presented with two ends of a single rope threaded around a baited table. Elephants waited for their partner before pulling, demonstrating understanding of cooperative roles.

PDF HTML
Using a modified cooperative apparatus, pairs of Asian elephants were presented with two ends of a single rope threaded around a baited table. Pulling one rope end without a partner causes the rope to unthread and renders the food unreachable. Elephants rapidly learned to wait up to 45 seconds for a delayed partner before pulling, demonstrating partner awareness on par with chimpanzees.

Vocal learning in the African elephant

Joyce H. Poole, Peter L. Tyack, Angela S. Stoeger, et al. (2005) Nature 434 455-456 DOI: 10.1038/434455a

We describe vocal imitation of truck noises by an adolescent African elephant in Kenya and Asian elephant vocal mimicry of human speech in South Korea, demonstrating that vocal production learning is not restricted to primates, cetaceans, and birds.

PDF HTML
Acoustic analysis shows that an African elephant living near a highway synthesized truck noise signatures in its night shelter, and an Asian elephant in a zoo mimicked Korean vocalizations spoken by its trainers. Vocal production learning provides an evolutionary mechanism for individualized greeting ceremonies and matriarch-led clan identifiers.

Social organization in the African elephant: the matriarch as repository of ecological knowledge

Cynthia J. Moss, Joyce H. Poole (1984) Animal Behaviour 32 (2) 538-548

Thirty years of demographic observation in Amboseli indicates that family units led by older matriarchs exhibit significantly higher calf survival rates during severe droughts, highlighting the survival value of institutional memory in long-lived mammals.

PDF HTML
Long-term records from Amboseli show matriarch age correlates directly with herd responsiveness to unfamiliar contact calls and distant thunder. Herds led by matriarchs over 50 years old navigate directly to historical dry-season springs unknown to younger generations, demonstrating cultural transmission of survival knowledge.
